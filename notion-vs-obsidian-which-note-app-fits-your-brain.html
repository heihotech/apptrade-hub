Notion vs Obsidian: which note app fits your brain

Picking a note-taking app sounds simple until you've spent three weekends migrating your entire life between tools and still feel vaguely unsatisfied. Notion and Obsidian are the two apps most people land on after outgrowing Apple Notes or Evernote — but they solve genuinely different problems. Getting this choice wrong costs you real time, so let's be precise about what each one actually does well.

The Core Philosophy Difference

Notion is a workspace. It thinks in databases, tables, and shared pages. Obsidian is a personal knowledge base. It thinks in plain-text files, local folders, and links between ideas. That single sentence explains roughly 80% of why people prefer one over the other.

If you want to run a content calendar, track job applications, and share a project brief with a client — all in one place — Notion is genuinely good at that. If you want to build a system of notes that surfaces unexpected connections between a book you read in 2019 and a problem you're solving today, Obsidian is the better tool.

Notion: Strengths and Honest Weaknesses

What Notion Does Well

  • Databases with multiple views — the same dataset can appear as a table, calendar, kanban board, or gallery. A content pipeline where you track status, publish date, and author in one place is genuinely impressive.
  • Collaboration — real-time editing, comments, and page permissions make it a legitimate team tool. Small agencies often replace Asana and Google Docs with Notion entirely.
  • Templates — the community template library is enormous. You can import a full CRM or meeting notes system in about 30 seconds.
  • Low learning curve for structured thinking — if you think in lists and tables rather than prose, Notion clicks immediately.

Where Notion Struggles

  • It can be slow, especially on mobile or with large databases. Pages with lots of embeds sometimes feel like loading a website from 2009.
  • Your data lives in Notion's cloud. Export exists, but it's messy and not something you'd do regularly.
  • Writing long-form content in Notion feels slightly off — the editor is functional but never quite gets out of your way.
  • The free plan is now fairly generous, but serious team features require a paid workspace.

Obsidian: Strengths and Honest Weaknesses

What Obsidian Does Well

  • Local, plain-text files — your notes are Markdown files on your own disk. You own them completely. Open them in any text editor, back them up however you like, version-control them with Git.
  • Bidirectional linking and the graph view — type [[meeting with Sara]] anywhere, and Obsidian creates a link. Over time, the graph of connected notes becomes a genuine thinking tool, not just a visual gimmick.
  • Plugin ecosystem — community plugins like Dataview (which lets you query your notes like a database), Templater, and Excalidraw turn Obsidian into almost whatever you need.
  • Speed — it opens instantly. Search across thousands of notes in milliseconds. No spinner, no loading state.

A practical example: a researcher keeping notes on 200 papers can tag each note with author, year, and topic, then use a Dataview query like this to surface everything on a theme:

```dataview
TABLE author, year
FROM #cognitive-science
SORT year DESC
```

Where Obsidian Struggles

  • The learning curve is real. Setting up sync, choosing a folder structure, and picking plugins takes time upfront.
  • Collaboration is essentially nonexistent without workarounds. It's a solo tool by design.
  • Sync between devices costs $8/month (Obsidian Sync) or requires technical DIY with iCloud, Dropbox, or Git.
  • No built-in database views — Dataview gets you there, but it's not beginner-friendly.

Free Alternatives Worth Knowing

If neither feels quite right, a few others are worth a look. Logseq is free, open-source, and local-first like Obsidian but uses an outliner structure instead of free-form pages — great for daily journaling and task tracking. AppFlowy is an open-source Notion alternative you can self-host, which appeals to anyone worried about data privacy. Joplin is a no-frills, free, open-source note app with solid sync options and end-to-end encryption.

Who Should Use Which

  • Use Notion if you work in teams, need databases and project tracking, or want one app to replace multiple tools like Trello, Google Docs, and Airtable.
  • Use Obsidian if you're a researcher, writer, or anyone who accumulates knowledge over years and wants to actually use it later — not just store it.
  • Use Logseq or Joplin if you want open-source and free without compromise.

Verdict

Notion and Obsidian aren't really competing for the same user. Notion is a productivity platform that happens to include notes. Obsidian is a thinking tool that happens to be very good software. If your work is collaborative and structured, pick Notion. If your thinking is personal and non-linear, pick Obsidian. The worst outcome is trying to force either one into a role it wasn't designed for — which, unfortunately, is exactly what most people do for the first six months.
