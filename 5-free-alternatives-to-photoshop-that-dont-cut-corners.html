5 free alternatives to Photoshop that don't cut corners

Adobe Photoshop is the industry standard for a reason, but at roughly $20–$55 per month depending on your plan, it's a hard sell for hobbyists, students, and freelancers watching their budgets. The good news: the free alternatives have genuinely caught up in most areas. A few of them will surprise you. Here are five tools worth your time — no bloatware, no paywalled basics, no embarrassing limitations.

1. GIMP — The Veteran That Keeps Improving

GIMP (GNU Image Manipulation Program) is the obvious first stop, and for good reason. Version 2.10 and beyond brought proper 16-bit and 32-bit color support, HDR imaging, and a non-destructive pipeline through GEGL. It handles layer masks, curves, channels, and smart selections in ways that feel familiar if you've used Photoshop for any length of time.

The interface takes adjustment — GIMP doesn't mimic Photoshop's layout, and some workflows like text handling and transformation feel clunkier. But for photo retouching, compositing, and batch processing via Script-Fu, it's genuinely capable. The Script-Fu console is worth learning:

(gimp-image-scale-full image 1920 1080 INTERPOLATION-LINEAR)

Best for: Power users comfortable with a learning curve who need a full-featured desktop editor.

2. Photopea — Photoshop in Your Browser

If you've ever opened Photopea and done a double-take, you're not alone. The interface is a near-replica of Photoshop's, right down to the toolbar positions and keyboard shortcuts. It opens and saves native .psd files without breaking layers or smart objects, which makes it genuinely useful for teams where only some members have paid subscriptions.

It supports adjustment layers, blend modes, Camera Raw filters, and even basic vector tools. The free version shows occasional ads — a small price for something this polished. Performance on large files can lag in the browser, so don't expect to edit a 500MB panorama smoothly.

Best for: Designers who need Photoshop compatibility on any machine, including Chromebooks or shared workstations.

3. Krita — Built for Artists, Surprisingly Versatile

Krita was built for digital painting, and it shows in the best way. The brush engine is legitimately one of the best available anywhere — paid or free — with textured, responsive brushes that illustrators and concept artists reach for over Photoshop's native tools. But Krita also handles photo compositing, layer styles, and masks with enough depth that it works as a general-purpose image editor.

  • HDR painting support
  • Non-destructive filter layers
  • Animation timeline (basic but functional)
  • Python scripting for automation
  • Excellent tablet pressure sensitivity out of the box

Where it falls short: it doesn't open .psd files as cleanly as Photopea, and it lacks the web/UI design features that make Photoshop relevant in those workflows.

Best for: Illustrators, concept artists, and anyone who does more painting than photo editing.

4. Darktable — The Lightroom Rival With Depth

Darktable is often compared to Lightroom rather than Photoshop, but that comparison undersells it. It's a non-destructive RAW processor with a module-based editing pipeline that includes tone mapping, color calibration, noise reduction, and a powerful masking system with parametric and drawn masks. Photographers who shoot RAW will find it handles files from Canon, Nikon, Sony, and Fujifilm cameras well.

The learning curve is steeper than Lightroom's, and the UI has its own logic that takes time to internalize. But once you understand how modules stack, the control is remarkable — comparable to what you'd pay for in Capture One.

Best for: Photographers who shoot RAW and want serious non-destructive processing without a subscription.

5. Pixlr E — When You Need Fast and Functional

Pixlr E (the more advanced of Pixlr's two editors) lives in the browser and strikes a middle ground between simplicity and real capability. It handles layers, blending modes, healing tools, and selection tools well enough for most marketing or social media work. The free tier is functional, though it pushes you toward the premium plan for some AI-powered features.

It won't satisfy a retoucher working on commercial beauty photography, but for someone who needs to put together a product banner or clean up a headshot in under ten minutes, it's fast and frictionless.

Best for: Non-designers and small business owners who need quick, competent edits without installing anything.

Quick Comparison at a Glance

  • GIMP — Most powerful desktop option, steeper curve
  • Photopea — Best Photoshop compatibility, browser-based
  • Krita — Best for illustration and painting
  • Darktable — Best for RAW photo processing
  • Pixlr E — Best for quick, casual edits

Verdict

None of these tools replicate Photoshop entirely — and they shouldn't have to. Each fills a specific niche well. If you're a photographer, start with Darktable. If you're a designer working in teams, Photopea will save you real money. If illustration is your world, Krita may honestly be better than what you're paying for. Try one before renewing that subscription.
