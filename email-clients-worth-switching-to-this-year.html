Email clients worth switching to this year

Your email client shapes how you start and end every workday. Yet most people stick with whatever came pre-installed — Gmail in a browser tab, or Outlook because IT said so. That's fine, but if your inbox feels like a chore, a better client can genuinely change the experience. Here's an honest look at the options worth your time in 2025, including a few solid free picks.

What to Actually Look for in an Email Client

Before downloading everything on this list, get clear on your priorities. Speed matters more than features for most people. After that, think about:

  • Platform support — do you need iOS, Android, Mac, and Windows covered?
  • Multiple account handling — mixing Gmail, Outlook, and a custom domain?
  • Privacy stance — does the app read your emails to train models or serve ads?
  • Keyboard shortcuts — a dealbreaker for power users
  • Price — some of the best clients cost $100+/year, which adds up

The Heavy Hitters

Mimestream (Mac only, free tier available)

If you live in Gmail and use a Mac, Mimestream is the one to try first. It's a native macOS app — not an Electron wrapper — so it's genuinely fast. It uses Gmail's API directly, which means labels, filters, and "Send & Archive" all work the way you'd expect. The free plan covers one account. Power users will want the $50/year Pro tier for multiple accounts and snooze features. The tradeoff: it's Mac-only and won't help if you use non-Google accounts.

Superhuman ($30/month)

Superhuman is aggressively good at one thing: speed. The keyboard-first workflow, the "Split Inbox," and the AI triage features are polished to an almost uncomfortable degree. But $360/year is a real commitment, and the onboarding call requirement feels like 2019. It's best for founders and executives whose inboxes are genuinely unmanageable. For most people, the price tag is hard to justify.

Apple Mail (free)

Underrated. On macOS Sequoia and iOS 18, Apple Mail finally added proper categorization — not just tabs, but actual intelligent sorting across Priority, Transactions, Updates, and Promotions. It handles multiple accounts well, respects your privacy by default, and costs nothing. If you're in the Apple ecosystem and don't need advanced snoozing or send-later scheduling, this is now a genuinely competitive option.

The Privacy-First Options

Proton Mail (free tier available)

Proton Mail is end-to-end encrypted by default, stored in Switzerland, and doesn't mine your data. The free plan gives you one address and 1GB of storage — enough to try it. The paid tiers start at around $4/month and bundle in Proton VPN and Calendar. The web interface has improved a lot; the mobile apps are solid. The limitation is that E2EE only applies when both sender and recipient use Proton. Emails to Gmail addresses are still encrypted in transit, not at rest on both ends. Best for journalists, activists, or anyone who's serious about communication privacy.

Tuta (free tier available)

Similar to Proton in philosophy, but Tuta encrypts subject lines too, which Proton doesn't by default. The UI is cleaner and the free plan is quite generous. Less name recognition than Proton, but technically competitive. Worth considering if you're starting fresh with a new email address rather than migrating an existing one.

Best for Teams and Power Users

Spark (free for individuals)

Spark is a strong all-rounder. The free plan is surprisingly full-featured: send later, snooze, templates, and email scheduling are all included. The team collaboration features — shared drafts, internal comments on emails — are genuinely useful for small agencies or startups that don't want to jump into Slack for every conversation. Works across Mac, iOS, Windows, and Android. The main concern is that Spark's servers process your emails to enable certain features, so it's not a privacy-first choice.

Thunderbird (free, open source)

Thunderbird got a meaningful redesign in 2024 and it's worth a second look if you dismissed it years ago. It handles IMAP, POP3, and Exchange accounts, supports a wide range of add-ons, and costs nothing. It's not glamorous, but if you want full control, offline access, and no subscription, Thunderbird delivers. Best for developers, system administrators, or anyone who's philosophically committed to open source.

The Verdict

There's no single best email client — it genuinely depends on your setup. Gmail users on Mac should try Mimestream before anything else. Privacy-conscious users should start with Proton Mail. Teams on a budget will find Spark's free plan hard to beat. And if you just want something reliable with no monthly fee, Thunderbird and Apple Mail are both better than their reputations suggest. Try one for two weeks before deciding — inbox habits take time to change.
