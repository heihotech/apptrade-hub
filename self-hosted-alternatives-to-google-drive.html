Self-hosted alternatives to Google Drive

Google Drive is convenient, no question. But handing your files over to a company that earns money from your data isn't for everyone. Whether you're a privacy-conscious individual, a small business with compliance requirements, or just someone who doesn't want a $9.99/month bill for cloud storage, self-hosting your own file sync solution is genuinely worth considering. The good news: the open-source ecosystem here is mature, and the options are better than ever.

What You Actually Need to Replace Google Drive

Before picking a tool, it helps to think about what you actually use Google Drive for. File sync across devices? Sharing links with collaborators? Editing documents in the browser? Photo backups? Most self-hosted alternatives cover file sync well, but some are stronger than others on collaboration or mobile support. Keep that in mind as you read through the options below.

The Main Contenders

Nextcloud – The Full-Featured Option

Nextcloud is the closest thing to a like-for-like Google Drive replacement. It's a full platform: file storage and sync, calendar, contacts, video calls, collaborative document editing (via Nextcloud Office, which runs Collabora or OnlyOffice under the hood), and a huge app ecosystem on top.

You can install it on a cheap VPS, a Raspberry Pi, or a spare desktop. The setup is more involved than the others on this list, but there's a well-maintained Docker image that makes it manageable:

docker run -d \
  -v nextcloud:/var/www/html \
  -p 8080:80 \
  nextcloud

Nextcloud's mobile apps for iOS and Android are solid, and the desktop sync client works reliably on Windows, Mac, and Linux. The main downside is resource usage — it can feel heavy on low-powered hardware, and performance tuning (Redis caching, background jobs) takes some reading to get right.

Best for: Teams or individuals who want a full Google Workspace replacement and are comfortable with a bit of sysadmin work.

Seafile – If Raw Sync Speed Matters

Seafile doesn't try to be everything. It focuses almost entirely on fast, reliable file synchronization, and it's noticeably snappier than Nextcloud for that specific task. It uses a chunk-based sync algorithm (similar to rsync logic) that only transfers changed parts of files rather than whole files — a real benefit if you're syncing large files or slow connections.

There's a built-in web interface, file versioning, and library-level encryption. Collaborative editing is available but feels like an afterthought compared to Nextcloud. The community edition is free; the Pro edition adds more admin features.

Best for: Users who prioritize raw sync performance and don't need the broader app ecosystem.

Syncthing – No Server Required

Syncthing takes a completely different approach: it's peer-to-peer, meaning your devices sync directly with each other without a central server. There's no web interface to log into, no server to maintain, and no single point of failure. Files stay on your devices and are encrypted in transit.

The tradeoff is that all your devices need to be online at the same time to sync. This works great for a home setup where you have a NAS or desktop that's always on, but it's awkward if you want to upload something from your phone and access it on a laptop that's been off for a week.

Best for: Technically comfortable users who want true decentralization and don't need a cloud-style "always available" setup.

Owncloud – The Nextcloud Predecessor

Nextcloud was actually forked from ownCloud back in 2016, and ownCloud has continued its own development path since. The modern ownCloud Infinite Scale (oCIS) is a complete rewrite in Go that's genuinely fast and cloud-native. It's worth a look, especially if you're running a containerized environment.

That said, the community around Nextcloud is larger, so finding help, plugins, and tutorials is generally easier.

Best for: DevOps-oriented teams comfortable with newer, less battle-tested software.

What About Storage Backends?

All of these tools can store files locally, but most also support external storage backends. Nextcloud and Seafile both support S3-compatible storage, which means you can pair them with something like Backblaze B2 or Wasabi for cheap, redundant object storage — and still control the software layer yourself. This is a sweet spot for a lot of people: self-hosted control plane, cloud-backed storage at a fraction of Google's prices.

Quick Comparison

  • Nextcloud – Most features, active ecosystem, heavier on resources
  • Seafile – Fastest sync, leaner footprint, less app variety
  • Syncthing – True P2P, no server needed, best for always-on local devices
  • ownCloud (oCIS) – Fast modern rewrite, smaller community than Nextcloud

Verdict

For most people making the switch from Google Drive, Nextcloud is the pragmatic choice — it covers the most ground and has the best documentation and community support. If you're a power user who just wants fast, no-fuss file sync without the overhead, Seafile is underrated. And if the idea of a central server makes you uncomfortable on principle, Syncthing is genuinely excellent once it's configured. None of these are plug-and-forget services, but the tradeoff — owning your own data — is worth it for many people.
